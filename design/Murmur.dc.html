3 The first week, and why Ask stays one question long
The Notebook age switcher above the phone in 1a is live — flip between day one, first week and four months in, and every screen changes. 3a is what the empty states say and why; 3b is the Ask question you asked about.
3a Day one is mostly absence. Dashed outlines, not cards. The app says what it's waiting for and then gets out of the way.
Sunday
On Sunday I'll read the week back to you. Until then this stays out of your way.
Nothing yet. After a week or so I'll start noticing what you keep coming back to, and they'll appear here on their own.
One note isn't much to ask questions of yet. Come back in a week — this gets useful somewhere around the fiftieth note.
The rules
Dashed, never solid. An empty state drawn as a filled card looks like a thing that failed to load. Drawn as an outline, it reads as a space reserved — which is the truth.
Say when, not what. Every empty state names the moment it fills — Sunday, a week, the fiftieth note. "No data yet" tells you nothing; "come back in a week" is a promise you can hold the app to.
Never ask for effort. No "add 5 notes to unlock", no checklist, no progress ring. The only thing that fills a notebook is living with it, and gamifying that is the fastest way back to being a work app.
Day one is allowed to be quiet. Today shows one note and a headline — "Your first note. That's the whole app, really." Nothing else. The onboarding already promised it would stay out of the way; this is the app keeping that promise on the day it's easiest to break.
The first Sunday letter is deliberately short and says so — "Four notes, which isn't many, so this will be short." Under-promising in week one is how you survive the comparison to week twenty.
3b One question, one answer. What we have now already is this — the discipline is in what we refuse to add.
What we built
Ask a question · get an answer · see the notes it read
Tapping the field clears it and starts over
No history, no follow-up field, no "regenerate"
Answer capped around 200 words
Sources always shown, always tappable
What a chatbot would be
A scrolling message thread you return to
"Tell me more" · "What about June?"
The model remembering what you just asked
Answers that end in a question back
An assistant with a name and a personality
The difference is who is doing the thinking. One question, one answer sends you back to your own notes — the sources are the point, and the answer is a route in. A conversation replaces your notes: after four turns you're talking to the model about your life instead of reading what you actually said.
It also protects the tone. Follow-ups are where a model starts performing — being encouraging, asking how that made you feel, offering to help you process it. Every one of those is a therapist impression, and it's the exact failure this product is defined against. A single answer has nowhere to drift.
And it keeps the cost honest. Conversation history compounds — turn five carries turns one through four. One-shot means every question costs the same two cents, which is what lets you not meter it and never charge a subscription for it.
The honest cost: some questions genuinely need a follow-up, and those users will re-ask with more words. That's a real trade, and I'd still take it — re-asking is a small friction, and becoming a chatbot is not a small one.

2 Dictate-into-a-note, and the two gaps I'd close next
2a is live in the prototype — open the pencil on Today, then Say it instead. 2b and 2c are the two things I think are actually missing, and I've deliberately stopped at two: everything else on my list made the app bigger without making it better.
2a Say it instead — dictation inside a written note. Not a mode switch: the same note, carrying on mid-sentence.
That the house was never the point. That I've been angry at a building because it's easier than being angry at her. What I want is to see her more, and every▍

Done
Why it's built this way
The dictated words come in tinted and settle to normal text when you tap Done. That tint is the whole design: it says this part wasn't typed, and you haven't read it yet. Without it, dictation errors get silently absorbed into something you believe you wrote.
It appends mid-sentence rather than starting a block, because the actual moment this serves is running out of patience with the keyboard halfway through a thought. A separate "voice attachment" would be a different, worse feature.
The audio is kept, not discarded — the note becomes a hybrid with an .m4a beside it. This is also why the label is "Say it instead" and not "Dictate": dictation implies the voice was a means to text. Here it's just the other way of talking.
Build note: on-device recognition only, streaming into the buffer at the cursor. No network, so it works on the tube.

Why this is the missing screen
Search assumes you remember a word. Folders assume you filed correctly at 7am on a walk. Neither is how anyone actually returns to a personal notebook — you come back wanting the thing about my sister, and you want to see how it moved.
This is also the only screen whose value compounds. Everything else is as good on day one as on day four hundred. A thread with fourteen months in it is something no notes app you're competing with can show, and it costs nothing extra — Claude is already emitting these names on every note.
No new navigation: thread chips already exist on the note detail and the recaps. Make them tappable. That's the entire cost.
The one-line arc at the top is a cheap Sonnet call over the thread's summaries, cached and regenerated only when the thread gains a note.
2c Gap 2 — you have to open the app. For a thought you have while walking, that's already too late.
Action Button
Press and hold. Recording before it's out of your pocket.
Lock Screen control
One tap, no Face ID, no unlock.
"Hey Siri, murmur"
The driving case. Hands never leave the wheel.
All three land in the same place: recording, transcribing on device, filed to Odds & ends until you say otherwise.
Why this outranks any screen
You named the two primary uses as walking and driving. In both, the current path is: get the phone out, unlock it, find the app, wait for it to load, tap record. That is five steps and about eight seconds, and the specific thoughts this product exists to catch do not survive eight seconds.
This is the difference between an app people admire and an app people use. It adds no screens, no navigation, and no clutter — it's entirely outside the app, which is the point.
It's also the cheapest thing on this page to build: an App Intent, a Control Center widget, and a Lock Screen control are roughly a day each, and the Action Button is free once the intent exists.
I'd put this ahead of 2b in the build order, and ahead of most of the polish in 1a.
Considered and deliberately left out
Tags, links, backlinks. Threads already do this without asking the user to do filing work while walking.
Streaks and stats. The fastest way to turn a notebook back into a work app. The heat map in 1e is the honest version — it shows habit without scoring it.
Ask your notes anything. Tempting, cheap to build, and it turns a notebook into a chatbot. The read-back is the better shape: it comes to you, unprompted, once a day.
Sharing and collaboration. The single clearest signal that a personal app has started becoming a work one.

Why
Five tabs is the shape of an app with a product team. A notebook has one surface.
